A photograph on a screen and the same photograph on paper are different objects, and the differences only become visible when you print regularly. Here is what a working home setup involves.

## The printer

Photo inkjets fall into two classes: dye-based printers, which are cheaper and produce vivid color with shorter print life, and pigment-based printers, which cost more and produce prints rated for decades. For work that will be sold or exhibited, pigment is the standard choice.

## The paper

Paper affects the final image as much as any camera setting. Matte papers mute contrast and deepen texture; baryta and luster papers hold deeper blacks and finer detail. Sample packs exist for exactly this reason — print the same image on five papers and the differences are immediate.

## Calibration

Most disappointing prints come from an uncalibrated screen, not a bad printer. A monitor at its factory brightness is far brighter than paper can ever be, so prints come out looking dark. Calibrating the display to a lower luminance and using the paper manufacturer's ICC profiles resolves most of it before any ink is wasted.
